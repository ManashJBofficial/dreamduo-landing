**Try one open-ended question a day, answer it both ways, then ask one follow-up.** For long-distance couples, a small detail about an ordinary day can open a better conversation than another round of “How was work?” Use the 30-day collection below over text, a voice note or a call. Five minutes is enough to start.

Start with “What's something you could talk about forever without getting bored?” for a light conversation, or “When do you feel most loved by me?” for something more personal. The collection moves through light topics, funny what-ifs, closeness, memories and future plans.

## How to use the 30-day challenge

Pick a starting day rather than waiting for the first of the month. Send one question when it suits your partner, with no expectation of an immediate reply. Take turns answering first so one person's response doesn't become the default.

After an answer, try **“What made you think of that?”** or **“Has that always been true for you?”** One follow-up usually gives you more to work with than sending three new questions. If a topic feels uncomfortable, skip it without asking for an explanation.

Don't turn missed days into a backlog. Resume with the next question whenever you're both up for it. This is a conversation aid, not a test of how much you care.

## Your 30 daily questions

**Days 1–6 are easy ways in.** Use them on a busy weekday when neither of you wants to explain your entire emotional state. A food opinion or a place you could spend hours can lead to a recommendation, a story or a future date idea.

**Days 7–12 leave room to be ridiculous.** Defend your answer, invent a scenario and let the conversation wander. You don't need to find a deeper meaning in your partner's vegetable choice.

**Days 13–18 ask about feeling close.** Listen for something you can actually do: asking a better follow-up, sending a voice note or remembering the detail they mentioned. An answer describes their preference today; it isn't a permanent rule.

**Days 19–24 revisit your shared history.** It's fine if you remember the same day differently. Tell the version you remember instead of deciding whose account is correct.

**Days 25–30 look ahead.** You can describe a dream without promising to make it happen immediately. If one idea interests you both, write down a small next step after the conversation.

## What if their answer is only a few words?

Offer something of your own before asking for more. If they say “the beach,” share the kind of beach day you imagined, then ask whether they mean a quiet morning or a busy seaside town. That gives the conversation a direction without making the short answer wrong.

You can also change the format. Someone who doesn't enjoy typing a long message might prefer a voice note on their walk home. Someone who finds a live call intense might enjoy thinking about a question first. Ask which feels easier instead of guessing that a short reply means disinterest.

If you're both drained, choose a lighter prompt or leave the conversation for another day. You don't have to rescue every quiet call.

## Turn one answer into something to do together

An answer about a city can become a shared place to research. A childhood hobby might suggest a drawing date. A detail about feeling loved can become one small change in how you check in.

For example, after the dream-home question, each choose one photo of a room you like and explain the detail you'd keep. You don't need a house deposit or a moving date to enjoy that conversation. ## Daily questions or a weekly check-in?

Use daily questions to explore each other's stories, preferences and ideas. Use a check-in to discuss how the relationship is working this week: what helped, what was difficult and what needs attention. A funny question can't replace a conversation you've both been avoiding.

## Common questions

### Do we need to talk every day?

No daily schedule is built into this challenge. Choose a pace you both want. One thoughtful exchange a few times a week may suit your schedules better than a rushed daily call.

### Can we answer these over text?

Yes. Send one prompt and your own answer, then leave room for theirs. Avoid sending the whole list as an assignment. Voice notes work well when typing feels like another task.
