**A useful couples goal gives both people a result they care about and a next step they can take.** “Go somewhere together” becomes “Choose a weekend trip by Sunday, then each find one place to stay.” Pick one example below and put it in the planner.

The examples start small on purpose. If something takes an afternoon, try it once before committing to a year of it.

## Twelve examples of shared goals for couples

### Things to do together

1. **Plan a weekend trip.** Each suggest one weekend and one place. By Sunday, agree on the date and destination before searching for accommodation.
2. **Try three local food spots in two months.** Save menus you both like and set the first date. Keep a backup if one needs a reservation you can't get.
3. **Make a movie list.** Add two titles each, check what you can both access, and set aside a night for the first one.
4. **Make a small photo album.** Choose 12 photos by the end of the month. Each pick three this week and write a sentence about one of them.

### Routines you want to keep

5. **Walk together once each weekend this month.** Pick the first route now and decide what to do if it rains.
6. **Learn one skill over six sessions.** Take a short beginner lesson together before signing up for a full course.
7. **Share two phone-free dinners a week for three weeks.** Pick evenings that work with both schedules and decide where you'll leave your phones.
8. **Test a fairer chore split for one week.** List the tasks, including buying supplies and arranging repairs. Each take ownership of one whole job and review how it felt on Sunday.
9. **Find one reliable long-distance call slot.** Compare work and sleep hours. Keep one regular call and a backup, then review after three calls.

### Plans that take several weeks

10. **Start a trip fund.** Choose a total and a booking date together. Record what you've already set aside before calculating what still needs to be saved.
11. **Finish one home project.** Agree on a room and what “done” looks like. Each do one thing before shopping, such as measuring a wall or listing materials you have.
12. **Build a small project together.** Pick something you can show a friend within a month, such as a recipe booklet or a page for a hobby. Agree on what goes in the first version.

## Pick the right kind of goal

- **Checklist:** Use steps when there's an obvious sequence, such as plans for a trip or home project.
- **Amount:** Use a numerical target when progress adds up, such as a trip fund.
- **Habit:** Count repetitions when you want something to happen regularly.
- **Time:** Record time spent when hours of practice matter more than the number of sessions.

Start with one way to measure progress. If your movie night needs a watchlist, checking off titles is enough.

## Copy this couples goal planner

1. **Our goal:** What do we both want?
2. **Why it matters:** What does each person get out of it?
3. **Finish line:** How will we tell when it's done?
4. **My next action:** What can I do, and by when?
5. **My partner's next action:** What will they handle, and by when?
6. **Our first milestone:** What's the smallest step worth noticing?
7. **Review date:** When will we decide whether to keep going or adjust?

## Worked example: a weekend away

**Goal:** Take a two-night trip next spring. **Finish line:** Agree on a destination, date and place to stay by the end of the month. One person checks possible dates by Tuesday. The other saves two places that fit their agreed limit by Thursday. On Sunday they pick a place or agree to keep looking. Their first milestone is confirming the dates, even if the trip is months away.

At your review, ask which action is still useful. If one of you can't get leave, find a new date before comparing more hotels.

## What to do if progress stalls

Bring one specific obstacle to the next review. “We still haven't agreed on a date” gives you something to solve. Ask whether you need a smaller next step, a different owner, or more time. If the trip is too expensive this season, agree on what to pause and pick a shorter outing you both still want to do.

If one person no longer wants the same goal, ask what changed. It may be easier to choose a new goal together than to keep assigning tasks for a plan only one person wants.
